# Slow play of "Violet"

*Violet* has a lot going for it. Central to the game is the titular Violet, a detailed, interesting character whose voice suffuses the game. The structure of the game appeals to IF nostalgia with a different take on the "my college campus" genre, in a sequence of well-simulated objects like a drawn-out Babelfish puzzle. The game layers on complexity, with surprises and a central theme of destructive puzzle solutions. Violet as the parser is thoroughly and admirably realised. But it felt a little off.

Perhaps it's the tone. Outside the office is a slowly-building war between pretend zombies and pirates, while you fiddle with a platypus-themed music player that turns your PC's head into a quirky UI. But then behind all this is the crushing realization that not only has the PC let Violet down, but multiple times, and well beyond the limits of what anyone should put up with. There's a sad desperation to it, of a life wasted, of a relationship wasted. And the relationship maybe shouldn't have even have happened, and what does this mean for the rest of your life? But then you'd get hit with a quirky joke and it's back to puzzles, baby!

But it can't be that because there's a lovely sense of push and pull, of stress and catharsis. It hits its pinnacle when you've sorted all the distractions plaguing you throughout this whole game... and then need to pee. It's pushing itself one step too far that makes it pushed just far enough.

Perhaps it's the puzzles. Every main solution is excessively complicated and bizarre in retrospect. But that's the charm of it, and it progressively builds, each time throwing up an obstacle but providing a nudge to the next step in the same breath. There are extensive help systems, guiding you the entire way without spoiling the experience. It's often obvious what you need to do, just not how. This is the mark of a good puzzle game.

Perhaps, scandalously, it's the narrator. Violet is front-and-center the entire game. Even in the ABOUT or CREDITS. The PC barely gets any detail, but Violet is finely sketched and there's no objection that the game is named after her.

Violet is Australian, but comes across as Australian-as-written-by-an-American. But the game knows it! And even comments on it. It is well aware of the narrative place it inhabits, with all the strengths and foibles. A running joke of Violet's self-censoring swearing becomes a character point later on, when she expresses her regret in moving to America and the mismatch of her dreams and reality.

I think, ultimately, Violet is a game of dynamic balance, like a unicycle where you need to keep moving back and forward to stay in one place. Too zany, or too serious, or too puzzley, or too straightforward, and it collapses into something that might still be good, but not IF-Comp-2008-winning good. Violet herself is the strong center it builds around, even with all the self-doubt, tension in the relationship and acknowledgement that this isn't even Violet, it's just an imagining of her. The target in motion is somehow a stronger target.

I went in expecting a neat gimmick and some fun collegiate puzzles, but came away having witnessed a successful type of IF structure I hadn't seen before.
