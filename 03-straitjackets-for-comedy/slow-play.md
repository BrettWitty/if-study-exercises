# Straightjackets for Comedy

## Play tasks

Take note of times where a traditional parser puzzle game might have required multiple specific verbs to solve a problem, but The Bat uses just one.

Note puzzles where the solution is about timing. Throughout there is a rising tension, but the game is actually quite forgiving.

Your other main verb is THINK which is an in-built hint system. Get a sense of how this plays with constraints and getting stuck.

A good joke subverts expectations right at the punchline. Note how The Bat does this with an unexpected verb. 

## My answers

1. I noted a number of verbs that "ATTEND TO" replaces: PICK UP, DROP, GIVE, SHOW, ASK, TELL, UNLOCK/LOCK, OPEN/CLOSE, CLEAN, FIX, TURN.

ATTEND TO has strict mechanics and complicated implementation. Picking up objects is limited by your two hands, and some objects require two hands to manipulate. Sometimes I think the simulation isn't too strict - some of the more physical interactions with the Master can be done with something in your hand.

Being a valet and dealing with a problem to the fullest extent with ATTEND TO also shaves off any parser simulation shenanigans. The constant reminder of your discretion and care is also a mechanical reminder that the game has got your back.

2. Despite the rising chaos and continual shifts in the party, I don't think there is anything that demands timing. You are forever told to attend to a thing at once, but really it's just your pressure on yourself. The donations 

One excellent bit of craftsmanship is how there is a frontier of problems that you have to deal with and at the right cadence, attending to one reveals a new one. It feels like a simulation, but it's not that free. You can't, for example, get the Baron involved in Dr Malatesta's bat capture. In this way each room is a little situation, but there's enough bleed-through via the character's chatter and your attempts to keep everything together whilst trying not to lose your main tools.

The Acts work well. There's the initial preparations to get your bearings and load a few Chekhov's guns. Then the building chaos of the guests arriving. Then the later game of attending to guests enough to get them to the performance (whilst slowly uncovering the theft plot). The short interlude also makes for a suitable breather in between acts.

So the game feels like it is about timing, but it's really about shape and positioning.

3. I regularly used THINK to keep on top of my tasks. Some of the tasks are very explicit in their requirements. Others less so. Near the end as you're accumulating tasks to find missing items, the THINK list is building narrative pressure, not mechanical. This is neat because sometimes at the end of a game like this, you're slowly sweeping up the last tasks and it feels like the game is shutting down rather than reaching a crescendo.

4. The compass hides an extra verb we've been using all along: QUIT. The modal nature of using the compass sets up the very ending as a nice aha moment. Without this, you'd almost never guess QUIT.
