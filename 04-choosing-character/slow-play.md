# Choosing Character instead of Story

# Slow play

I didn't know about Crème de la Crème before I played it. I had it recommended to me by Brian Rushton aka mathbrush as an exemplar of Choice of Games' style of IF.

The game is a Choicescript boarding school adventure, complete with study pressures, scandals, and... engagements?! Gallatin College is a finishing school for high-class individuals, so your family's history and etiquette are deemed much more important than, say, mathematics or languages. There is also a strong element of finding your mate, in whatever form that might take. Your parents were once high-class but have fallen, so you need to navigate what that means for you and your future.

You get a considerable amount of choice in your character. I played first as a studious young man, mostly focussed on academics but not at all averse to the idea of romance if it happens to wander his way. Close to the entirety of the first chapter of twelve is choosing all the different components of your character. Their name, their gender, their pronouns, how they did at school, their relationship to their parents... I kept a record of all the choices I made, up to about 30 when I stopped keeping track.

This thankfully isn't done via some initial barrage of character sheets. The introduction unrolls through a familiar story of you catching the train to your new college, bumping into your new classmates and getting the lay of the land.

The game is incredibly flexible and you can choose the gender/pronouns of most of the supporting characters, although I didn't do this first up. I let the game choose randomly. Even with a whole class of decisions taken off my plate, the first chapter is exhausting. Every section is a short paragraph or so, often with a half-dozen options on how your character reacts.

This is where I hit a peculiar kind of slipperiness. It's rather common in IF (especially Choice of Games-style IF, I believe) to decide upon the reality of the situation by the choices your character makes. There is no objective reality until you decide it. I mean, that's the idea of the "interactive" part of "interactive fiction". You the player are making choices about the character, which establishes facts retroactively but is also how the story plays out. This makes sense as an interaction, but it's often muted in the games I usually play. I wasn't too bothered about the choices establishing my character --- it's much more enjoyable than a pre-game questionnaire --- but the fact that almost everything was up for grabs started to feel unsettling.

There's quite a number of supporting characters, and most can be any sex or gender. Even when the game chooses for you, there was a slipperiness of avoiding pronouns for some characters, or keeping descriptions and characterisations very minimalist. I had the feeling of walking through a quantum cloud of possibilities, the wave function just barely collapsing to keep the story going. A character's sexuality or romantic proclivity dictates story, so these are mostly fixed per character. However it's not revealed to the player until much later into the relationship so it feels like this too could be flexible.

Add into the mix the world being a semi-timeless fantasy Europe with analogues for the "main" countries, but with their own geography and history briefly mentioned. Enough to differentiate them, but not enough to situate them in my brain. I was screaming out for something to be fixed by the author. A rock I could hold onto.

I applaud having a diverse cast, especially a specific main character from a vast array of possibilities. But this non-particular form of writing, combined with a substantial volume of independent choices inside a fairly tight tunnel of actual story, somehow encouraged me to ignore the diversity and latch onto the few legible traits of each character, which felt like stereotyping. It was a very alien feeling in my brain.

And it's good writing! These issues arise because the author is taking care of very many other things simultaneously. All the introductions and world-building, all the little seeds it needs to sow for the later chapters. As a loose analogy, it feels like realising an author has constrained themselves so they can't use the letter E. If you're into the story, you won't notice. But maybe you do notice, and it's maddening that they don't use the letter E. That one slightly peculiar word choice could easily be avoided with the letter E, but they don't! It's fantastically impressive that they're sticking with this constraint, but surely there's another way!

The next two chapters introduce you to more characters (who seem to be much more prescribed), begin the school year and start to introduce the overarching plot of Crème de la Crème, a battle between schools as to who is the most... high-class? By the end of the third chapter I was struggling. This is what you can play for free.

I almost stopped it there, but felt that would be uncharitable. I pressed on and am glad I did. The boarding house drama started to fall into place and the choices from before started paying off. My character (Felix Moretti) had an accidental flirt with the local mystic girl, Delacroix. Gonzalez seemed interested but then revealed they weren't at all interested in anyone, anytime. Unperturbed, Felix joined the prefects and started pursuing the very proper lady Hartmann. Somewhere in there was a school rivalry, some heroic academic efforts and a scandalous mystery that he somehow got wrapped up in. In fact, everything else was clicking so nicely that I was slightly annoyed by the forced distraction of mystery plot. I needed to see if I could romance Hartmann! Or the hottie from the other school, Auguste! My character got penalised for basic schoolwork but dropped a nuclear bomb on the exams, which seemed to be my skeleton key for the rest of the game.

I came to understand the whole structure a lot more. Every chapter is a large number of brisk scenes, some branching, some essential. Overall there is a set menu of narrative events, but some of them are not important to your character, and others are vital. You don't get a huge number of chances to follow any particular strand, but each seems to be honoured. Even though there is a staggering amount of text, I wished you could sit with it a little longer. I had to choose who I wanted to try to get engaged to, but felt like I had just started pursuing them.

This briefness of individual plotlines means that the game has to get more explicit later on. Out of a menu of options, I chose to let Delacroix down, stating I wasn't interested in her. This plays out in a very perfunctory scene. I was given the same for Hartmann. I think I kept my options open for Auguste, but she didn't really appear for the rest of my story. I understand that these interactions were short and blunt to help tame some of the larger complexity, and the author invested their words elsewhere.

The end of the game deals with the usual angst of where everyone will end up in their near future. I got into the prestigious university, despite my family being broke. I got to finish the game with Hartmann... who didn't want to get engaged but might give me hand squeezes if no-one was looking. WTF.

I did play through a second time, with a wildly different character: a bon vivant bisexual nonbinary called Quinn (just Quinn). I chose different approaches and got to see quite a few new scenes, including one from a radically different perspective from my first guy involving a fire evacuation. Quinn was there to have fun, romance everyone and mess things up. This time I chose the specifics of the supporting characters, making my canonical choices.

This second playthrough was more enjoyable as the initial chapters were less of an aimless grind, and I could appreciate when the author was setting things up for later, whereas first time around it seemed like Yet Another Random Plotline.

As for the rest of the exercises, I began to write a short game in ChoiceScript in this style of choosing your character and the story happens regardless. It took a while to separate writing for character versus story. I had to rein in my instinct of "different character choice means I have to arc off in a vastly different story" and present the same general scenes but let you play them out differently.

This instinct continued to confuse me as I thought it was analogous to my game Anne of Green Cables where you can make choices that change interpretations of scenes, but otherwise don't significantly dent the story. But I think Anne is a railroad and Crème de la Crème is a tunnel. That might sound the same, but there's a larger volume of freedom in the latter. In Anne you can perhaps route through a few different junctions, but it's more-or-less the same path. In Crème de la Crème you're stumbling through a wider space of possibilities, perhaps even avoiding some scenes accidentally or intentionally. There should be a number of strands for the player character to inhabit throughout the game. What's important is not just the moment-to-moment choices, but how those compound into meaning and relationships across the game. Crème de la Crème offers deep replayability, demonstrating their mastery of this idea. I hope to learn it properly.
